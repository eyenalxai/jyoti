What the room remembers

By A. Moreno · 12 minute read · Subscribe

A screen is a window with no weather. We spent a month watching light move across a small apartment and learned that the room notices more than we do.

The first thing to go was the lamp. It had been a reliable object for six years, a warm circle on the ceiling that arrived before anyone spoke. Then the screen grew larger and the lamp began to look like a second opinion. At dusk the room was blue; at noon it was white. Nobody had decided this. It simply happened, the way a room fills with the colour of the thing everyone is looking at.

We measured the wall behind the sofa at seven in the evening. The reading was not the colour of the paint, nor the colour of the film. It was a third colour, made by the room itself. This is the part that is easy to miss: light does not arrive; it accumulates. A wall is a slow instrument.

The problem with an average

The obvious approach is to average the screen. The average knows about quantity and nothing about attention. A white document with a blue link averages to white, which is correct, but a night scene with one red window averages to near-black, which is useless. The average is honest and slightly stupid. It answers a question nobody asked.

A neutral scene should produce a neutral light. This sounds obvious until you build a system that is very good at finding the one saturated thing in a white room.

The alternative is to ask what the eye is doing. The eye is not a sampler; it is a narrator. It finds the face, the fire, the lit window, and it lets the rest fall away. A screen full of text is mostly paper to the eye, even when the paper is a dark editor and the text is a small bright animal. See the method notes and the frame archive for the raw captures.

Three ways of looking

  • Dominant colour. Group the pixels, weigh the groups by size and saturation, and keep the hue that survives. This is the baseline.
  • Background. Ignore the edges and the faces; average the least salient part of the frame. This is the ambient reading.
  • Histogram. Count hues in perceptual bins and take the mode. This is the vote.

None of them is a theory of attention. They are three different ways of being wrong, and the work is to choose the wrongness that feels right in a dark room. The fire should be orange. The document should be white. The ocean should not turn the ceiling teal because a wave caught the light for half a second.

At the end of the month we turned the lamp back on and left it there. The screen still changes. The room still remembers. But now there is a rule, written on a sticky note by the door: when in doubt, be neutral.
